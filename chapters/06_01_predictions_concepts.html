From Models to Intervention Predictions

Graduate Quantitative Methods | Class discussion

What you should know after reading this component:

  1. Explain the difference between an ordinary conditional prediction and a prediction under a specified intervention.
  2. Estimate a sample-average contrast in R by predicting outcomes for every individual (in the dataset) under two exposure values.
  3. Explain why an estimated intervention contrast is not evidence of a causal effect (at least, not by itself).

Recall: From estimand to adjustment to model

flowchart LR
  E[Estimand] --> A[Adjustment Set]
  A --> M[Model]
  M --> P[Intervention predictions]
  P --> R[Effect estimate]

Once again, you must define the estimand before choosing variables, and choose variables before fitting a model.

From causal questions to predictions

1. Total effect

Question:
What is the total effect of predator abundance on nesting success?

Prediction:
> Higher predator abundance will lead to lower nesting success.

2. Mediation

Question:
Is the effect mediated by parental behavior?

Predictions (directional):

Higher predator abundance will lead to reduced parental provisioning (or increased vigilance).

Reduced provisioning will lead to lower nesting success.

Therefore, predator abundance will reduce nesting success partly through its effect on parental behavior.

After accounting for parental behavior, the remaining (direct) effect of predator abundance on nesting success will be smaller in magnitude.

3. Effect modification

Question:
Does the effect vary with environmental conditions?

Prediction:
> The negative effect of predator abundance on nesting success will be stronger under certain environmental conditions (e.g., low canopy cover or high rainfall).

Key idea

Mediation predictions must follow the direction of each causal link, not just the overall association.

What exactly are we predicting?

Ordinary conditional prediction: \(\widehat{E}(Y\mid X=x,C=c)\): predicted outcome for observations with specified values of X and C.

Intervention prediction: \(\widehat{E}(Y\mid do(X=x))\): predicted population outcome if X were set to x, provided the causal assumptions needed to identify this quantity hold.

The same fitted regression model may be used to calculate both kinds of prediction. Changing newdata does not, by itself, establish a causal interpretation.

Our causal DAG

flowchart LR
  C --> X
  C --> Y
  X --> Y

C is a common cause of X and Y. For the total effect, C must be accounted for; a mediator should not simply be added as an adjustment variable.

Discuss: If we fit Y ~ X + C, why isn’t a regression coefficient automatically an estimate of every causal question we could ask?

A population-average contrast

For two interventions \(x_0\) and \(x_1\), predict every observation’s outcome under each intervention, retaining its observed C:

\[ \widehat{\Delta}(x_1,x_0)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left[\hat m(x_1,C_i)-\hat m(x_0,C_i)\right]. \]

This is regression standardization, a simple form of g-computation. It averages over the empirical distribution of C.

Think before coding

  • What changes between the two hypothetical datasets?
  • What stays the same?
  • Why not simply set C to its mean?
  • Does an intervention on X also intervene on C?
