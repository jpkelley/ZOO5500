12  Hypotheses and Predictions

12.1 Hypotheses and Predictions

12.1.1 Exactly what is a hypothesis?

Scientists regularly use the word hypothesis, but its meaning is surprisingly slippery. Students are often taught that a hypothesis is a specific, testable, and falsifiable statement about the natural world. Consider the following statement from ecology:

Increasing food availability increases reproductive success.

This is readily recognized as a hypothesis because it suggests an obvious empirical test (and potential for falsification). But what about the much broader and over-arching Food-Limitation Hypothesis that posits that food availability limits the abundance or population dynamics of organisms?

Using only this broad wording, no single study can test the proposition in its entirety; it is simply too complex. Researchers instead test more specific propositions about food limitation, such as whether:

  • experimentally increasing food availability increases reproductive success;
  • food supplementation increases adult or juvenile survival;
  • greater food availability increases breeding probability or clutch size;
  • food availability increases parental provisioning or offspring growth;
  • seasonal variation in food availability affects recruitment or population growth; or
  • the effects of food availability differ among species, habitats, seasons, or life-history stages.

Each one of the above is a specific scientific proposition generated by the broader Food-Limitation Hypothesis. This does not mean, however, that only the more specific propositions qualify as hypotheses. Both the broad proposition and the more specific propositions are hypotheses. The problem is that scientists use hypothesis to describe propositions at very different levels of explanation.

The word hypothesis derives from the Greek ὑπόθεσις (hypóthesis), from hypo- (“under”) and thesis (“placing,” “position,” or “proposition”). In its most basic form, it means a statement placed underneath; that is, a proposition that forms the basis for future reasoning. Originally, it did not inherently mean a narrowly specified statement amenable to a single empirical test. This brings us to an important distinction that dominates the field of ecology:

A hypothesis does not have to be directly testable by a single study.

Broad hypotheses, such as the Food-Limitation Hypothesis, make general claims about the natural world. To test them, researchers develop more specific hypotheses that can be evaluated with particular observations or experiments. Thus, testability comes from making a broad hypothesis specific enough to connect it to evidence.

For example, consider three hypotheses at different levels of specificity:

  • Broad organizing proposition: Food availability limits avian populations.
  • Causal hypothesis: Increasing food availability increases reproductive success.
  • Mechanistic hypothesis: Increasing food availability increases reproductive success through increased parental provisioning.

No single hypothesis in the above list is inherently a “better” hypothesis simply because it is more specific or more readily testable. These are all scientific propositions about food limitation, but here is the important thing to keep in mind: they are not equivalent claims. The first encompasses many possible causal relationships and mechanisms. The second specifies one causal relationship. The third specifies a particular causal mechanism. More generally, hypotheses can make different kinds of claims: some concern patterns or states of nature, others concern statistical relationships, and still others make explicit claims about what would happen if one part of an ecological system changed.

Specificity does not turn a non-hypothesis into a hypothesis; it turns a broad hypothesis into a more precise one. Broad hypotheses can organize entire research programs; narrower hypotheses allow particular components of those broader ideas to be confronted with evidence. Later in the course, we will see that these distinctions matter because the second might lead us to target a total effect, whereas the third directs our attention toward an indirect effect.

12.2 Common forms of ecological hypotheses

Hypotheses differ not only in how broad or specific they are, but they also differ in the kind of claim they make. Some of these claims concern patterns or states of nature. Others concern statistical relationships. Still others make explicit causal claims about what would happen if one part of an ecological system is changed.

The following are common forms encountered in ecology:

Hypothesis type Form Example
Descriptive X has a particular state, pattern, or distribution. Nest predation accounts for a large proportion of observed nest failures.
Associational X is associated with Y. Rainfall is associated with foraging activity.
Directional associational Higher X is associated with higher/lower Y. Higher rainfall is associated with lower foraging activity.
Causal X affects Y. Rainfall affects foraging activity.
Directional causal Increasing X causes Y to increase/decrease. Increasing rainfall reduces foraging activity.
Mechanistic / mediation X affects Y through M. Rainfall reduces reproductive success through reduced food availability.
Effect modification The effect of X on Y depends on Z. The effect of rainfall on foraging activity depends on habitat heterogeneity.

These categories are not mutually exclusive. For example, consider this hypothesis:

Increasing rainfall reduces reproductive success through reduced food availability.

This hypothesis is simultaneously causal, directional, and mechanistic. Similarly, several forms of ecological hypotheses are special cases of effect modification:

  • Buffering: Z weakens the effect of X on Y.
  • Amplification: Z strengthens the effect of X on Y.
  • Group-specific effect: the effect of X on Y differs between groups A and B.

12.2.1 Some things called “hypotheses” contain way more than a hypothesis

Scientific hypotheses sometimes appear as If-Then statements. However, an If-Then statement can contain both a hypothesis and a prediction derived from it. For example, consider the following statement:

If increasing rainfall reduces foraging activity, then birds experiencing greater rainfall will spend less time actively foraging.

Formally, this is a conditional statement with two parts:

  • Antecedent (hypothesis): Increasing rainfall reduces foraging activity.
  • Consequent (prediction): Birds experiencing greater rainfall will spend less time actively foraging.

Thus, the entire If-Then statement is not necessarily the hypothesis. In this example, the antecedent states the hypothesis, while the consequent states a prediction that follows from it. In other cases, this pattern may not be so obvious. This is why we should not identify hypotheses by their grammatical form. A hypothesis does not have to be written as an If-Then statement. Also, an If-Then statement is not necessarily a hypothesis. The more useful question is:

What scientific proposition is being asserted, and what role does that proposition play in the argument?

Throughout this course, we will therefore focus less on whether a statement looks like a hypothesis and more on identifying the claim about the ecological system that the researcher intends to evaluate.

12.3 Verbiage of descriptive, associational, and causal hypotheses

The forms above describe several ways that hypotheses can be structured. It is also critically important to recognize whether scientific inference –yours or that in a published paper– aligns with the intended hypothesis. At a broad level, we can distinguish three kinds of scientific claims:

  • Descriptive hypotheses: what patterns or states exist
  • Associational/predictive hypotheses: how variables statistically relate or predict one another
  • Causal hypotheses: how changing one variable would produce changes in another

Each of these categories tends to use characteristic language. Recognizing these linguistic features can help reveal what type of inference an author intends to make. It can also expose mismatches between the hypothesis, analysis, and eventual conclusions (exceedingly common in ecology). The lists below are not exhaustive.

WarningDescriptive Hypotheses

Verbs: describe, characterize, document, quantify, summarize, report, measure, compare, catalog

Nouns: pattern, distribution, trend, rate, frequency, abundance, composition, diversity, structure, variation

NoteAssociational / Predictive Hypotheses

Verbs: correlate, predict, associate, relate, forecast, explain variation in, covary with, model, estimate

Nouns: correlation, association, relationship, covariance, linkage, dependence, prediction, forecast, model, estimate

TipCausal Hypotheses

Verbs: affect, influence, cause, determine, alter, drive, generate, produce, lead to

Nouns: effect, impact, influence, mechanism, process, pathway, driver, cause, consequence, outcome

WarningBeware of causal language

Words such as effect, influence, driver, and mechanism are not merely colorful, hyperbolic alternatives to association. They imply something about how the system responds to change. Using causal language creates a causal burden; that is, the analysis must support the type of claim being made. We will spend much of this course learning how to determine whether it does.

12.4 Hypotheses are not predictions

A hypothesis and a prediction play different roles in scientific reasoning.

Hypothesis Prediction
Role Proposition about how the ecological system works Expected empirical consequence of a hypothesis
Scope Generalizes beyond a particular study Tied to a particular study, design, or measurement
Example Increasing rainfall reduces foraging activity. During 10-minute observations, birds experiencing higher rainfall intensity will spend less time actively foraging.

A prediction therefore connects a hypothesis to what we expect to observe in a particular study. For example, we can progressively move from a broad scientific idea toward something that can be directly connected to data:

  • Broad hypothesis: Food availability limits avian populations.
  • More specific causal hypothesis: Increasing food availability increases reproductive success.
  • Prediction: Food-supplemented territories will produce more fledglings during the breeding season than otherwise comparable unsupplemented territories.

The prediction connects the hypothesis to a particular study design and measurement process.

ImportantThe wording alone does not make something a hypothesis or prediction

A statement such as

Higher rainfall will be associated with lower foraging activity.

could function as either a hypothesis or a prediction.

Its role depends on the scientific argument in which it appears.

If it states the proposed relationship about the ecological system, it functions as a hypothesis.

If it states the empirical pattern expected because of another hypothesis, it functions as a prediction.

Thus, hypotheses and predictions are distinguished primarily by their logical role, not by whether they contain words such as if, then, will, increase, or decrease.
